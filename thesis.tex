\title{Hybrid Topic and Domain-Adaptive Modelling for Financial Risk and Forecasting}

\author{Sarthak Govind Vishnu}

\date{\today}

\submityear{2026}

\abstract{
This project asks a single, falsifiable question: does the text of a firm's SEC Form~10-K Item~1A ``Risk Factors'' disclosure add incremental predictive value for its forward realised volatility, beyond lagged volatility and a set of standard structured financial characteristics? The work began as an encoder-construction exercise: domain-adaptive pretraining, a novel three-view contrastive objective, and a BERTopic axis over Item~1A filings. Once that pipeline showed that a count-based bag-of-words model out-predicted every learned encoder, the project was reframed as a strict, forward-looking volatility-prediction benchmark, in which the domain-adapted encoder became a means to answer the question rather than the product itself. A structured feature block grounded in the asset-pricing literature is built as the baseline the original pipeline never had, and every text representation is evaluated as an increment on top of it, under a fixed prediction head and paired statistical tests across a twelve-year walk-forward backtest. No encoder configuration beats a full-text TF-IDF representation under any leakage-audited protocol, including a clean-cutoff retrain of a task-supervised encoder. The defended model is therefore structured features plus full-text TF-IDF under a sparse ridge head, carrying a small, consistent, statistically suggestive text increment. Three extensions test how far this conclusion generalises. Relabelling the task across eight horizons from three to ninety trading days shows the model ranking is horizon-robust while the text increment itself is single-peaked at seven days, attaining conventional significance there. Earnings-call tone is informative at the call date but is absorbed by the time of the filing, a horizon-contrast finding rather than a simple null. Conditioning the text increment on pre-filing insider-trading activity shows it concentrates in firms whose insiders disagree, a design that locates where in the cross-section the increment is earned rather than only whether it exists on average. A bounded pilot further finds that a generative language model's own reading of the insider-trading record is statistically indistinguishable from a small set of hand-crafted features, and that its scores are themselves unstable across repeated identical queries, evidence obtained directly rather than argued from precedent alone. The study contributes an evaluated rather than asserted benchmark, a plausible account of why count-based text remains competitive out of sample, a persistence finding, a null result on disclosure change, a term structure for the text increment, and a design for locating where and when a text signal is earned.
}

\chapter{Introduction}

Corporate disclosures contain dense, forward-looking information about firm-level risk, yet this information remains under-exploited in quantitative finance. The dominant textual approaches, namely finance-specific sentiment lexicons \cite{loughran2011liability} and general-purpose language models, were not designed for the specialised vocabulary of risk disclosure, where words such as \textit{liability}, \textit{uncertainty}, and \textit{exposure} carry precise domain meanings that general encoders systematically misrepresent. A natural response is to build a better encoder, and the original design of this project did exactly that: domain-adaptive pretraining (DAPT) \cite{gururangan2020don} on Item~1A text, a three-view contrastive objective extending the dual-view framework of Chiu et al.\ \cite{chiu2025financial}, and a BERTopic topic axis \cite{grootendorst2022bertopic} for interpretability.

That pipeline was carried through in full. Over its phases it produced a domain-adapted encoder, three contrastive variants and a 49-topic BERTopic model, and a downstream ablation that scored each text representation (the encoders, their topic exposures, and a bag-of-words baseline) on predicting a firm's 30-day forward realised volatility. Its headline result, however, pointed away from the encoder it was built to showcase: the count-based representation won. A TF-IDF bag-of-words model matched or beat every one of the learned encoder conditions, and the natural first reading was that volatility prediction from risk-factor text is a lexical task, in which the frequency of specific risk language carries the signal while dense semantic embeddings blur it.

Read at face value, this is a statement about text representations alone. A closer examination against the finance literature on the determinants of volatility suggests a more careful interpretation. The entire comparison was conducted among models whose only non-text predictor was a single lagged-volatility number. The asset-pricing literature, however, establishes a set of structured firm characteristics as the first-order drivers of volatility. A comparison between text representations that all omit these dominant predictors cannot, on its own, establish whether disclosure text is valuable. This observation motivates a change of question. The relevant question is not which text representation ranks firms best, but the one standard in empirical finance: \emph{does Item~1A text add incremental predictive value for forward realised volatility, beyond lagged volatility and standard structured financial controls?} Volatility is strongly autocorrelated, so the informativeness of any new variable is judged by whether it improves prediction \emph{after} controlling for what is already known. Under this framing the encoder is no longer the product, but one candidate text representation among several, to be evaluated against a strict, forward-looking benchmark.

Answering the reframed question required two things the original pipeline lacked: a structured financial baseline (the asset-pricing characteristics that are the first-order determinants of volatility) and an evaluation protocol tight enough to measure the small increments that ``incremental value over a strong baseline'' necessarily involves. Building both, and running an exhaustive, leakage-audited ablation over them, produced a clear and somewhat surprising verdict. The structured baseline alone outranks the entire original text-only pipeline; text adds a small, consistent, statistically suggestive increment on top of it; and, decisively, no dense-encoder configuration beats a simple full-text TF-IDF representation under any admissible protocol, including a twelve-year walk-forward backtest and a clean-cutoff retrain of a task-supervised encoder. The defended model of the study is therefore structured features plus full-text TF-IDF under a sparse ridge head.

That verdict raised a further, natural question. If the increment is real but small, how far does it generalise, and can it be sharpened by asking not only \emph{whether} text adds value but \emph{when} and \emph{for whom}? The study was therefore extended along three further axes, each a test of the same incremental-value question rather than a new one. Relabelling the forecasting target across eight measurement horizons asks whether the 30-day headline result is an artefact of that particular choice of window. Attaching a second text modality, earnings-call tone, asks whether the increment is specific to the 10-K or reflects a more general fact about forward-looking corporate language. Conditioning the text increment on pre-filing insider-trading activity asks whether the increment is uniform across firms or concentrated in a identifiable subset of the cross-section. A bounded pilot on generative language models closes the study by asking whether a fundamentally different route to feature extraction, prompting rather than encoding, is worth pursuing at all, and answers that question with evidence rather than with argument alone.

The contributions of this report are: an evaluated benchmark, in which the finding that TF-IDF wins survives an exhaustive grid of challengers across twelve years of backtests rather than a single comparison; a plausible account of why the count model wins, tying the observation that dense encoders do not transfer forward to the well-established idea of concept drift, stated as the best-supported interpretation of the evidence rather than as a demonstrated mechanism; a persistence finding, that year-stale risk text predicts volatility almost as well as current text; a null result with an explanation, that disclosure-change features do not help volatility prediction; a term structure for the text increment across eight measurement horizons; a horizon-contrast finding for a second text modality, earnings-call tone; a design for locating where in the cross-section a text increment is earned, using pre-filing insider-trading activity as the conditioning variable; and a negative result on generative feature extraction obtained directly from a bounded pilot rather than argued from precedent. Every one of these findings is, underneath, an answer to the same question stated above: not which representation, anchor, or firm subset ranks best in the abstract, but how much each adds over what a structured, forward-looking baseline already knows. The following chapter situates the work against the relevant prior literature and states the study's research questions precisely; the chapters after that describe the methodology, the results, and the discussion and conclusions in turn.

\chapter{Background}
\label{sec:sota}
This study sits at the intersection of seven literatures: (1) the structured cross-sectional predictors of volatility, (2) text-based prediction of volatility from 10-K filings, (3) sentence-representation learning and its financial adaptations, (4) the informativeness of disclosure \emph{change}, (5) the methodology of leakage-free financial forecast evaluation, (6) information asymmetry and insider trading as an observable proxy for it, and (7) the feasibility of generative language models as feature extractors. Table~\ref{tab:survey} summarises the principal works; the technique-level citations that support specific design choices (RoBERTa-style packing \cite{liu2019roberta}, SimCSE \cite{gao2021simcse}, supervised contrastive learning \cite{khosla2020supervised}, LoRA \cite{hu2022lora}, SEC-BERT \cite{loukas2022finer}, Longformer windowing \cite{beltagy2020longformer}) are introduced where they are used in Chapter~\ref{sec:method}.

\textbf{Structured volatility predictors.} The asset-pricing literature establishes a compact set of firm characteristics as first-order determinants of the cross-section of volatility. The persistence of realised volatility across horizons is captured by the HAR-RV model of Corsi~\cite{corsi2009simple}, whose in-sample one-day-ahead $R^2$ (0.565 for USD/CHF, 0.707 for the S\&P~500, 0.236 for Treasury bonds) far exceeds the sub-2\% typical of return prediction; volatility is a far more forecastable quantity than returns. Ang, Hodrick, Xing and Zhang~\cite{ang2006cross} give the canonical cross-sectional treatment of market beta and idiosyncratic volatility, and establish size, leverage, book-to-market and momentum as the standard control set; book-to-market and size originate with Fama and French~\cite{fama1992cross}. Amihud~\cite{amihud2002illiquidity} defines the illiquidity ratio, and Conrad, Dittmar and Ghysels~\cite{conrad2013ex} price higher moments (skewness, kurtosis). Collectively these define the structured feature block of Section~\ref{sec:struct}, and, crucially, the baseline against which the value of text must be measured.

\textbf{Text-based 10-K volatility prediction.} Kogan et al.\ \cite{kogan2009predicting} established the reference method for this exact task: bag-of-words features from 10-K filings with a linear model, predicting post-filing return volatility. Loughran and McDonald~\cite{loughran2011liability} showed that finance-calibrated word lists predict returns, volume and volatility better than general dictionaries, which is why TF-IDF is treated here as a \emph{strong} lexical baseline rather than a naive one. Campbell et al.\ \cite{campbell2014information} provide the cleanest empirical anchor for the choice of text source, showing that Item~1A risk-factor disclosures are reflected in post-disclosure idiosyncratic and systematic risk, so they are informative rather than boilerplate. Magner et al.\ \cite{magner2025decoding} are the nearest prior work, using Loughran--McDonald and GPT-based sentiment on Item~1A to predict index returns; the domain-adapted, benchmark-validated approach here is a methodological advance over dictionary and prompt-based sentiment on the same section.

\textbf{Representation learning and financial adaptation.} General-purpose sentence encoders built on BERT \cite{devlin2019bert}, such as SBERT \cite{reimers2019sentence} and E5 \cite{wang2022text}, are insensitive to financial risk vocabulary, while token-level financial models such as FinBERT \cite{yang2020finbert} target sentiment rather than risk-level similarity; together these motivate domain adaptation \cite{gururangan2020don}. The closest published precedent, Chiu et al.\ \cite{chiu2025financial}, applies dual-view contrastive adaptation to Item~1A filings; this project adds a third, inter-firm sector view. FinMTEB and Fin-E5 \cite{tang-yang-2025-finmteb} advance financial sentence embeddings but rely on a 7B-parameter backbone, and, tellingly for this study, the FinMTEB benchmark reports that bag-of-words representations \emph{outperform} dense embedding models on financial semantic-similarity tasks. Count-based text beating dense text on financial data is therefore a documented phenomenon, not an idiosyncrasy of this pipeline. BERTopic \cite{grootendorst2022bertopic} supplies the interpretable topic axis, and Jehnen et al.\ \cite{jehnen2026fintextsim} (FinTextSim) demonstrate the sentence-transformer-plus-BERTopic pattern on 10-K disclosures. A separate strand trains text encoders \emph{end-to-end} on a volatility target (Qin and Yang \cite{qin2019you}, Yang et al.\ (HTML) \cite{yang2020html}), and forms the precedent for the task-supervised encoders evaluated in Chapter~\ref{sec:results}. Sawhney et al.\ (VolTAGE) \cite{sawhney2020voltage} extend the same task-supervised approach to earnings-call tone specifically, and is the closest precedent for the second text modality of Section~\ref{sec:results-calls}.

\textbf{Disclosure change.} Cohen, Malloy and Nguyen~\cite{cohen2020lazy} (``Lazy Prices'') showed that year-over-year changes in 10-K text predict returns, with the alpha arising from investor under-reaction to the changes that occur. Kravet and Muslu~\cite{kravet2013textual} report the volatility-side analogue, that annual increases in risk-disclosure volume are associated with higher return volatility. These motivate the disclosure-change branch of Section~\ref{sec:results-change}.

\textbf{Evaluation methodology.} López de Prado~\cite{de2018advances} is the reference for why $k$-fold cross-validation is inappropriate for financial time series and for walk-forward evaluation and lookahead bias (Chapters~7, 11 and~12). Diebold and Mariano~\cite{diebold1995comparing} provide the forecast-accuracy comparison test used throughout, and Grinold and Kahn~\cite{grinold2000active} give the benchmark that contextualises information-coefficient magnitudes. Lipton and Steinhardt~\cite{lipton2019troubling} articulate the ablation principle, namely isolating the true source of empirical gains, that governs the fixed-head protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. Lu et al.\ \cite{lu2018learning} formalise the concept-drift framework used to interpret the out-of-period results.

\textbf{Information asymmetry and insider trading.} Frankel and Li~\cite{frankel2004characteristics} use the profitability and intensity of insider trades as an observable proxy for a firm's information environment, which is the precedent for using insider activity as a conditioning variable in Section~\ref{sec:results-insider} rather than as a trading signal in its own right. Cohen, Malloy and Pomorski~\cite{cohen2012decoding} classify insider trades as routine or opportunistic based on whether the same insider traded in the same calendar month in prior years, a classification adapted for the opportunistic-fraction feature of Section~\ref{sec:insider-method}. Bettis, Coles and Lemmon~\cite{bettis2000corporate} document that the great majority of firms operate formal pre-filing blackout policies restricting insider trading, which grounds the construct-validity check reported alongside the insider-trading feature block. Shalen~\cite{shalen1993volume} models dispersion of beliefs among informed traders as a direct driver of both volatility and trading volume, which is the theoretical anchor for reading the insider-disagreement conditioning result of Section~\ref{sec:results-insider} as a cross-sectional counterpart to the study's absorption reading of earnings-call tone.

\textbf{Generative language models as feature extractors.} A distinct way to use language models, prompting a generative model to score text on interpretable dimensions rather than encoding it, is untested by the encoder-based pipeline of this study, and Section~\ref{sec:llm-pilot-method} runs a bounded pilot to assess it directly. Sclar et al.\ \cite{sclar2024quantifying} find open-source language models extremely sensitive to prompt formatting choices that are explicitly meaning-preserving, with performance differences of up to 76 accuracy points from typesetting the same question differently. Ouyang et al.\ \cite{ouyang2025empirical} find that identical requests to the same model disagree on a large majority of code-generation tasks, and that fixing the decoding temperature to zero reduces but does not eliminate this non-determinism; the finding is cited here for the decoding mechanism it establishes rather than for a magnitude, since the domain is code generation rather than numeric scoring of financial text. Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2024large} show that a language model used as an evaluator can be made to reverse its own ranking of two candidate responses simply by reordering them, evidence that language-model-based scoring is sensitive to factors that are irrelevant to the content being scored, in a pairwise-comparison setting adjacent to but distinct from the absolute scoring task of Section~\ref{sec:llm-pilot-method}. Together these three results motivate treating an elicited language-model score with caution as a candidate feature, a caution the pilot of Chapter~\ref{sec:discussion} tests directly on this study's own data rather than relying on precedent alone.

\section{Research gap and research questions}
\label{sec:gap}
The residual gap this project targets is precise: an Item~1A-specific study that (a) builds the structured baseline the text-only literature omits, (b) poses the question as one of \emph{incremental} value over that baseline, and (c) measures it under a strictly forward-looking, leakage-audited protocol with paired statistical tests and an ablation that isolates one difference at a time. The extensions described in the introduction each widen that same gap along one further axis: whether the answer depends on the horizon at which volatility is measured, whether it depends on which text source is asked, whether it depends on which firm is asked, and whether a different family of language model would answer it more cheaply.

\subsection{Research questions}
The study is organised around one primary research question and four subsidiary questions, each answered in Chapter~\ref{sec:results}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Primary.} Does Item~1A risk-factor text add incremental predictive value for a firm's forward realised volatility, beyond lagged volatility and a set of standard structured financial controls, once every feasible learned text representation has been given a fair chance to demonstrate it?
\item Is any such increment specific to the 30-trading-day horizon at which it was first measured, or does it hold, and take the same shape, across a wider spectrum of measurement horizons?
\item Does a second text modality, earnings-call tone, add value on top of the 10-K text, and if the two sources disagree, at which point in time does the disagreement resolve?
\item Does the text increment hold uniformly across firms, or does it concentrate in an identifiable part of the cross-section, conditioned on a firm's pre-filing information environment as measured through insider-trading activity?
\item Can a generative language model extract usable, stable volatility-relevant features from raw event data at lower engineering cost than a hand-built feature set, or does it face obstacles, such as elicitation instability or backtest contamination, that a hand-built feature set does not?
\end{enumerate}

    \caption{The core pipeline. \chapter{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the core pipeline that this chapter describes. Reading it from left to right, a data-and-label stage (Ph.1) assembles the Item~1A corpus, the volatility labels, and the temporal split, and feeds two parallel feature families. The first is a structured financial block of standard volatility predictors. The second is a set of text representations: a domain-adapted encoder (Ph.2, DAPT), its three-view contrastive variants (Ph.3), additional and task-aligned encoders, a windowed-encoding step that turns each filing into a dense vector, a BERTopic axis (Ph.4) that yields topic exposures, and a disclosure-change branch. The two families meet at the fusion step, where each text representation is added on top of the structured baseline and passed to a fixed prediction head (Ph.5, ablation). The resulting models are evaluated first on a single forward split, and then under an expanding-window backtest with an admissibility audit and a clean-protocol retrain, which together produce the defended benchmark and its verdict. Three further sections describe the extensions that answer research questions 2 to 5 of Section~\ref{sec:gap}: a horizon-relabelling procedure (\S\ref{sec:horizon}), a second text modality (\S\ref{sec:calls-method}), a third data source (\S\ref{sec:insider-method}), and a bounded generative-feature-extraction pilot (\S\ref{sec:llm-pilot-method}). Stages unchanged from the original design are described in full so the chapter stands alone; where the extended study altered or added a stage, the change is stated explicitly.

\section{Problem formulation and target}
\label{sec:target}
The task is to predict a firm's forward realised volatility from the text of its Item~1A ``Risk Factors'' disclosure and its structured characteristics. Volatility is the standard deviation of daily log returns over the trading days strictly after the filing date, annualised by $\times\sqrt{252}$; the lagged measure uses the same number of trading days strictly before. The modelling target is $\log$ forward volatility, because volatility is approximately log-normal and strictly positive, and using the log everywhere keeps error metrics comparable across models. The headline results of Chapters~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:discussion} are stated at a 30-trading-day window, a horizon close to, but not identical with, the one-month convention of the realised-volatility literature: Corsi's HAR-RV model~\cite{corsi2009simple} defines its monthly component as 22 trading days, so the 30-day choice is this study's own design decision rather than an inherited convention. Section~\ref{sec:horizon} describes how that choice is tested for robustness rather than merely asserted.

Two metrics are reported throughout. The \textbf{information coefficient (IC)} is the within-year cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation between predicted and realised volatility; it answers whether the model ranked firms correctly from least to most volatile within a year. $\mathbf{R^2_{\log}}$ is the coefficient of determination on the log level. Ranking is the primary lens because the economic use of such a model is cross-sectional, while the level is dominated by market-wide conditions. Magnitudes must be judged against a persistence baseline, not against zero: Grinold and Kahn~\cite{grinold2000active} note that an IC of about 0.06 already implies a top-decile information ratio for \emph{return} forecasting, while Corsi~\cite{corsi2009simple} shows volatility is far more forecastable than returns, so within-year volatility ICs of 0.5--0.6 are the relevant range.

\section{Data and labels}
\label{sec:data}
The universe is the S\&P~500, including delisted firms to avoid survivorship bias. The text corpus is one Item~1A section per filing, approximately 8{,}247 filings spanning roughly 2006 to 2026; the master feature table has one row per filing (about 8{,}105 rows), keyed by ticker and fiscal year. Filing dates are indexed from EDGAR, and firm identifiers are linked across the SEC and finance worlds: \textbf{CIK--PERMNO} for volatility-label assignment, \textbf{CIK--SIC} (2-digit) via the EDGAR submissions API for sector-view pair construction, and a fiscal-year index aligning filing dates to return windows. Daily prices come from two sources in priority order: the FINSABER S\&P~500 price file (2000--2024, including delisted names), and WRDS CRSP daily returns for 2025.

The temporal split, used identically by every stage, is: training on filings dated before 2025-01-01 (about 7{,}353 filings), validation on 2025 filings (406 filings, of which about 397 have complete labels), and a held-out 2026 test year whose forward windows had not closed at the time of the experiments. All model selection and fitting uses the training years only. As a reusable research artefact, and to keep the representation module separable from the prediction head, a firm-filing feature table is assembled that joins the identifiers, the dense and topic representations, and the lagged and forward volatility labels into one ML-ready record (schema in Appendix~\ref{app:schema}). Two further data sources are joined to this panel for the later extensions: earnings-call transcripts (\S\ref{sec:calls-method}) and Form~4 insider-transaction records (\S\ref{sec:insider-method}), both matched to filings under the same leakage discipline as the core panel.

\subsection{The structured feature block}
\label{sec:struct}
The structured block contains the standard cross-sectional volatility predictors, each grounded in the asset-pricing literature: realised volatility at horizons of 21, 63, 126 and 252 trading days (the multi-horizon persistence of HAR-RV \cite{corsi2009simple}); market beta and idiosyncratic volatility \cite{ang2006cross}; firm size, leverage and book-to-market \cite{ang2006cross,fama1992cross}; Amihud illiquidity \cite{amihud2002illiquidity}; return skewness and kurtosis \cite{conrad2013ex} (kurtosis as an exploratory control); momentum \cite{ang2006cross}; and vol-of-vol, drawdown and dollar volume. Fundamentals (log market capitalisation, leverage, book-to-market, return on assets) come from Compustat. Coverage is approximately 99\% across 7{,}666 filings. Building this block involved no new modelling, only feature engineering, which is precisely the point: it is the baseline any volatility study should start from, and the one the original text-only pipeline omitted.

\section{Text-representation modules}
\label{sec:text}

\subsection{Domain-adaptive pretraining (DAPT)}
Following Gururangan et al.\ \cite{gururangan2020don}, the encoder was first adapted to financial-risk language by continued masked-language-model (MLM) training on the 10-K corpus. The base model is \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} rather than raw \texttt{microsoft/mpnet-base}, because the sentence-transformer variant already has useful sentence-level geometry; only the encoder (not the MLM head) is carried forward, so the trade-off is to reshape its token vocabulary toward financial risk while preserving that geometry. The main design decision was how to chunk long Item~1A prose (median around 7{,}000 words) into 512-token sequences. Two within-document, non-overlapping greedy packers in the RoBERTa DOC-SENTENCES family \cite{liu2019roberta} were built: a sentence-aware packer, and a paragraph-aware packer that never splits an individual risk factor. This choice is supported by RoBERTa's ablation and its replication under tighter compute by Geiping and Goldstein \cite{geiping2023cramming}, and by the domain precedents LEGAL-BERT \cite{chalkidis2020legal} and SEC-BERT \cite{loukas2022finer}. An A/B comparison on an identical validation set selected the paragraph-aware variant (validation perplexity 2.2278 against 2.2671), with the recorded caveat that the margin is small and partly confounded by the paragraph variant taking about 22\% more gradient steps at a fixed five epochs. Training used the standard recipe: 15\% masking, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$, effective batch 32, five epochs with early stopping on validation perplexity, fp16, 512-token sequences. The selected checkpoint reduces validation perplexity by about 16.5\% relative to \texttt{microsoft/mpnet-base} (2.23 against 2.67).

\subsection{Three-view contrastive fine-tuning}
The post-DAPT encoder was fine-tuned with a SimCSE-style InfoNCE objective with in-batch negatives \cite{gao2021simcse}, extending the dual-view framework of Chiu et al.\ \cite{chiu2025financial}, which pairs 256-token risk paragraphs. Three positive-pair views were constructed: a \textbf{lexical} view (two overlapping spans of the same paragraph), a \textbf{chronological} view (the same firm's matched risk factor in adjacent years, requiring TF-IDF cosine at least 0.5), and a new \textbf{sector} view (paragraphs from different firms in the same 2-digit SIC industry and fiscal year). Numbers and dates are normalised to \texttt{[NUM]} and \texttt{[DATE]} tokens following SEC-BERT \cite{loukas2022finer}. The loss is InfoNCE at temperature 0.05 \cite{gao2021simcse} with label-aware false-negative masking on the sector label \cite{khosla2020supervised}, and the soft sector view is down-weighted by 0.5. Pairs are built from the training split only. Three encoders were trained: \texttt{dual} (lexical + chronological, a replication of Chiu et al.), \texttt{three} (adding the sector view, full fine-tuning), and \texttt{three\_lora} (the sector variant with LoRA adapters, $r=16$ \cite{hu2022lora}). Each encoder was also scored intrinsically on the FinMTEB STS and retrieval sub-tasks \cite{tang-yang-2025-finmteb}, and the intrinsic scores are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:eval-tables}. They show the expected pattern: DAPT improves in-domain semantic similarity but damages retrieval geometry, which is the known anisotropy of raw MLM embeddings, and contrastive fine-tuning repairs it. A diagnosis of the sector view found that its random within-industry pairing produced many near-unrelated pairs. These pairs blurred the embedding geometry under full fine-tuning but were contained under LoRA.

\subsection{Additional and task-aligned encoders}
To make the encoder comparison exhaustive, three further encoders were added to the grid beyond the contrastive family. \texttt{sbert} is the off-the-shelf SBERT sentence transformer \cite{reimers2019sentence}, specifically the \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2}} with no further adaptation. \texttt{bge} is BAAI's BGE model \cite{xiao2023cpack}, specifically the \texttt{bge-base-en-v1.5} checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5}}, a modern strong general-purpose embedder included to address the objection that the original encoders were simply not good enough. Two \emph{task-aligned} encoders test whether aligning the objective with the target closes the gap. \texttt{volaware} is a contrastive encoder whose positive pairs are paragraphs from firms in the same within-year forward-volatility decile. \texttt{ftvol} is fine-tuned end-to-end on the volatility regression target itself, following the task-supervised precedents \cite{qin2019you,yang2020html}. A leakage-clean retrain of \texttt{ftvol}, called \texttt{ftvol2018}, is introduced in Section~\ref{sec:results-robust}.

\subsection{Windowed encoding}
\label{sec:windowed}
TF-IDF ingests the full filing by construction, so the encoders are given the same complete input through a windowed encoding step. Each long paragraph is split into overlapping 256-token windows with a stride of 192 tokens, every window is encoded, and the resulting window vectors are mean-pooled to a 768-dimensional paragraph vector before the usual paragraph-to-filing pooling. This produced 462{,}590 windows over 176{,}616 paragraphs in total. Segment-encode-pool is the established treatment of documents that exceed a transformer's context window \cite{beltagy2020longformer}, with the financial-NLP precedent of hierarchical transcript encoding \cite{yang2020html} and the 256-token paragraph unit of Chiu et al.\ \cite{chiu2025financial}.

\subsection{BERTopic topic extraction}
To provide the explanatory axis, BERTopic \cite{grootendorst2022bertopic} was fitted on the training paragraphs of each encoder's embedding space, with UMAP (15 neighbours, 5 components, cosine metric), then HDBSCAN (minimum cluster size 200), and a class-based TF-IDF topic representation reduced to 49 topics. The model is fitted on training documents only and applied to validation and test documents, so no held-out text shapes the topic space. Each filing receives a 49-dimensional topic-exposure vector (the fraction of its paragraphs assigned to each topic). Topic coherence ($C_V$) sits between 0.67 and 0.72 across encoders. The DAPT-only backend was excluded because its anisotropic embeddings prevent UMAP from converging.

\section{Robustness across measurement horizons}
\label{sec:horizon}
Every result described so far is stated at one horizon, realised volatility over the 30 trading days after the filing, and Section~\ref{sec:target} already notes that this is the study's own design choice rather than an inherited convention. To test whether the study's conclusions are specific to it, labels were regenerated across a spectrum of seven further horizons, 3, 5, 7, 10, 20, 60 and 90 trading days, under the identical definition (standard deviation of daily log returns over the window strictly after, and for the lagged predictor strictly before, the filing date, annualised), from the same price sources as the structured features. The spectrum begins at three trading days rather than one for a reason of construct rather than convenience: the label is a sample standard deviation, undefined for a single observation, and answering the one-day case would require substituting a different estimator (the absolute return, scaled by the annualisation factor) partway along the curve. One estimator applied consistently across the whole spectrum is worth more than one additional point on it.

The construction was validated before any new horizon was read, by requiring it to reproduce the study's existing 30-day labels exactly, to rounding precision, on every comparable filing. Windows are also required to lie adjacent to the filing date, so a firm whose price history has ended receives no label rather than a stale one. The fair comparison of Section~\ref{sec:results-robust}, structured persistence against \texttt{structured [ridge]} against \texttt{struct+tfidf [sparse]}, was then rerun at each horizon, with the lagged predictor matched to the same horizon, over both the 2018--2024 expanding-window backtest and the 2025 validation year.

\section{Earnings-call tone features}
\label{sec:calls-method}
A second text modality, the earnings call, is imported into the same incremental-value protocol used throughout the study: the question is not whether call tone predicts volatility in isolation, which prior work already establishes \cite{qin2019you,yang2020html,sawhney2020voltage}, but whether it adds signal over the structured baseline and the 10-K text already in the panel. The construction proceeds in two stages. A full transcript history of 39,501 unique earnings-call transcripts, spanning 2006 to early 2025, was obtained and matched to filings at two distinct anchors. At the \emph{call anchor}, a call's tone is tested against the 30-day volatility following the call itself. At the \emph{filing anchor}, tone features are attached to each filing from the most recent call strictly preceding the filing date, a leakage-free construction by design since only information available before the filing date is used. The match succeeds for 88.3\% of the panel overall and at least 95\% in every backtest year. Both anchors are evaluated under the same fixed-head, paired-test protocol as every other text representation in the study (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), and both are re-run across the full horizon spectrum of Section~\ref{sec:horizon} to test whether a finding at one anchor is an artefact of the 30-day label rather than a property of the anchor itself.

\section{Insider-trading features}
\label{sec:insider-method}
A third information source, insider transactions reported on SEC Form~4, is added not as another candidate text representation but because it speaks directly to \emph{where} in the cross-section a text increment is earned. Insider trading is a standard observable proxy for information asymmetry \cite{frankel2004characteristics}, which is itself a volatility construct: it measures how much material information is concentrated in few hands, not which direction that information points. Every feature is accordingly framed as intensity or dispersion rather than as a directional trading signal. Eight features are computed per filing: transaction and distinct-insider counts, net direction by count and by value, insider disagreement (the fraction of trading insiders on the minority buy-or-sell side of their firm's window), the officer sell fraction, abnormal trading intensity (the window trade rate against the firm's own trailing 365-day rate), and the opportunistic fraction, adapted from Cohen, Malloy and Pomorski's~\cite{cohen2012decoding} routine-trade classification: a trade is scored opportunistic unless the same insider traded in the same calendar month in at least three prior years, a deliberately looser criterion than their original test, since the three qualifying years need not be consecutive or confined to a fixed look-back window.

Features aggregate a trailing window from 180 to 3 days before each filing date. The three-day margin covers Form~4's statutory two-business-day disclosure deadline (Sarbanes-Oxley \S403, codified at 15 U.S.C. \S78p(a)(2)(C)), so every feature used is genuinely public at prediction time. Matching is by CIK with a ticker fallback, and coverage is effectively complete: every panel filing falls inside the Form~4 universe, in every year from 2006, so these features are valid over both backtest windows, unlike the calls family. As a construct-validity check, median abnormal trading intensity is mildly negative in every year, consistent with the pre-filing blackout policies that most firms are documented to operate \cite{bettis2000corporate}.

Two questions are asked of this feature block, both described fully in Section~\ref{sec:results-insider}. The first is the standard level question: does adding the insider block to the structured model improve prediction on its own? The second, the question the block was built for, is conditional: does the text increment established in Chapter~\ref{sec:results} concentrate in the high-insider-activity half of each year's cross-section? Because the conditioning variables are computed strictly pre-filing, splitting each test year's firms at the median value of a conditioner is knowable at prediction time, so the conditioning analysis is leakage-free by construction. This design, conditioning a text increment on an insider-trading state to locate where in the cross-section that increment is earned, combines standard ingredients (a pre-filing conditioner, a paired-by-year test) in a way no precedent was found for in the literature review behind this study, and the results in Section~\ref{sec:results-insider} are read with that in mind.

\section{Generative feature-extraction pilot}
\label{sec:llm-pilot-method}
The encoder-based pipeline of Sections~\ref{sec:text} to~\ref{sec:insider-method} represents every text and event source by training or fine-tuning a model on it. A distinct route, untested by any of the encoders above, is to prompt a generative language model to score raw event data directly on interpretable dimensions and use those scores as features. Chapter~\ref{sec:discussion} states why this route was ultimately scoped out of the main study. This section describes a bounded pilot run to test that judgement against evidence rather than assert it on argument alone, with its stopping rule fixed before any score was read.

The pilot scores the same insider-trading record that the eight hand-crafted Form~4 features of Section~\ref{sec:insider-method} summarise. An open-weights instruction model was prompted to score each of 865 insider-trading windows on three zero-to-hundred dimensions, five independent times per filing under a fixed prompt, fixed model version and fixed decoding seed, so that any dispersion in the resulting scores reflects the elicitation process itself rather than an uncontrolled source of variation. To keep the pilot's scope strictly separate from the contamination concern raised in Chapter~\ref{sec:discussion}, only 2024 and 2025 filings, both post-dating the model's training cutoff, were scored, and every prompt was identifier-stripped: no ticker, company name, insider name, or absolute date, only day offsets relative to the filing date and pseudonymised insider labels. The model's three scores were then compared against the eight hand-crafted features under one prediction head and one set of cross-validation folds, using the same five-fold within-year design applied to the earnings-call filing-anchor evaluation of Section~\ref{sec:calls-method}, for the same reason: a score available only on the evaluation rows cannot be learned under a train-before-year split. As a control, the identical pilot was also run on prompts that retained the real identifiers, to check whether removing them changed anything on this post-cutoff slice, where no look-ahead effect should be present to find.

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
Every condition is evaluated on the same data: one row per firm and filing year, so that models are always compared on identical inputs. Each condition is built by starting from the structured feature block and, where relevant, adding one text representation on top (TF-IDF, an encoder embedding, topic exposures, change features, call-tone features, insider-trading features, or a combination of these). This step-by-step design is referred to as an ablation ladder, because each rung adds exactly one feature block to the structured baseline, and the resulting IC increment can be attributed to that one addition. Because the quantity of interest is a small increment on a strong baseline, three rules ensure that every comparison isolates exactly one difference, and they apply uniformly to every extension in this chapter, not only to the core text representations.

\emph{First}, the prediction head is held fixed within every comparison. When the model class and the feature set change at once, credit assignment is arbitrary \cite{lipton2019troubling}, so both a ridge regression and a gradient-boosted tree (HGB) are reported for every condition, and increments are always quoted between same-head arms. \emph{Second}, every text representation receives the full text, via the windowed encoding of \S\ref{sec:windowed}, so a comparison between representations is not a comparison between the amounts of text each was allowed to read. \emph{Third}, statistical comparisons are paired: within each evaluation year the models predict the same firms, so per-year ICs are compared as matched pairs across years, and per-firm squared errors are compared by Diebold--Mariano tests \cite{diebold1995comparing}. The robustness extension of Section~\ref{sec:results-robust} adds the expanding-window (walk-forward) backtest that is standard for financial prediction \cite{de2018advances}, and Section~\ref{sec:results-insider}'s conditioning test adds a pre-registered directional hypothesis, fixed before the conditioning results were read, as a further safeguard against post-hoc interpretation.

\section{Compute used}
\label{sec:compute}
All training and evaluation ran on the University of Edinburgh's Teaching cluster under SLURM, using a different GPU configuration for each stage's memory and throughput needs. Domain-adaptive pretraining, the three contrastive fine-tuning runs, and the FinMTEB intrinsic evaluation ran on a full NVIDIA RTX~A6000; the FinMTEB evaluation alone took about 49 minutes on this GPU. The vol-aware contrastive encoder (\texttt{volaware}) used a full NVIDIA H200. Lighter single-GPU jobs, namely the supervised \texttt{ftvol} fine-tune, the full-text windowed re-encoding of the corpus, and the BERTopic fit, ran on an H200 partitioned as a smaller 1g.18gb MIG slice, while the more demanding clean-protocol \texttt{ftvol2018} retrain (20 epochs, batch size 128) used a larger 3g.71gb MIG slice of the same card. The Phase~5 ablation and the walk-forward backtest work on pre-computed feature vectors rather than raw text, so these ran on CPU-only nodes with no GPU allocation. The horizon spectrum, the insider-trading conditioning tests, and the generative-feature-extraction pilot reuse this same infrastructure: the first two are CPU-only extensions of the existing ablation and backtest code, and the pilot's inference calls are the only additional GPU workload the extensions introduce.

GPU jobs were budgeted up to 48 hours of wall-clock time, with a shorter 12-hour budget for the lighter FinMTEB and encoding jobs. BERTopic's UMAP and HDBSCAN clustering step is CPU-bound even when scheduled on a GPU node: an earlier attempt to fit UMAP over the DAPT-only embedding space was terminated after roughly ten hours without converging, which is the practical reason the DAPT-only backend was excluded from BERTopic in Section~\ref{sec:text}.

\section{Responsible research including ethics}
All data are sourced from publicly available, regulatory-mandated SEC filings (EDGAR), aggregated market price feeds (CRSP and FINSABER), and publicly available earnings-call transcripts. Form~4 insider-transaction records (\S\ref{sec:insider-method}) are themselves a public, statutorily mandated SEC disclosure, so no additional consent or access concern arises from their use, and the generative pilot of \S\ref{sec:llm-pilot-method} strips company and insider identifiers from its prompts as a further precaution beyond the data's already-public status. No personal data beyond what firms and individuals are already required to disclose publicly is used, no human subjects are involved, and no informed consent or IRB review is required. CRSP data are accessed exclusively through WRDS under the University's institutional licence and are not redistributed or retained beyond the project period.

\section{How to read the tables}
Every results table uses these columns. \textbf{IC} is the within-year cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation (the primary metric). \textbf{95\% CI} is the bootstrap confidence interval on that single-year IC; with $n=393$ firms the interval spans roughly $\pm 0.06$, which is why single-year differences of 0.01--0.02 can never be conclusive on their own. $\mathbf{R^2_{\log}}$ is the coefficient of determination on the log level. \textbf{DM $p$} is the $p$-value of a Diebold--Mariano test \cite{diebold1995comparing} comparing the row's squared errors against the reference on the same firms. Condition names state the feature blocks and the head in brackets, so \texttt{struct+tfidf [sparse]} is structured features plus the TF-IDF block under the sparse ridge head.

This grid also settles a question left open by the original pipeline. After the first, text-only ablation, it was suggested that a task-supervised encoder, trained directly on the volatility target, would outperform the other encoders. That encoder (\texttt{ftvol}) was built and tested here, and it reaches parity with the count model but does not beat it. Stated in incremental-value terms, the grid says one thing throughout: no dense representation adds more over the structured baseline than the count model does, and the generic encoders add essentially nothing over it at all.

One caveat applies to this table and affects everything that follows in Section~\ref{sec:results-robust}. The parity rows come from encoders whose training data included volatility labels from before 2025, the exact period being evaluated. The \texttt{ftvol} checkpoint was also selected using its performance on the validation year itself. Both practices let information about the evaluation period leak into training, so these rows overstate what the encoder could do on genuinely unseen data. They are useful as exploratory evidence but should not be read as a fair result. Section~\ref{sec:results-robust} removes this leakage and reports what a task-aligned encoder can really do.

\section{The robustness extension}
\label{sec:results-robust}
Everything here goes beyond the original evaluation design, and is a deliberate methodological adaptation: the single-year comparison cannot statistically separate models 0.01 apart, and, more seriously, it cannot detect a class of leakage that turned out to be present.

\subsection{The expanding-window backtest}
The protocol is the standard walk-forward design for financial prediction \cite{de2018advances}. For each test year $Y$ from the start year to 2024, every model is trained on filings from years strictly before $Y$ and scored on year $Y$, exactly as a deployed model would have been. Two windows are reported, 2013--2024 and 2018--2024; the $t$-statistic is the mean yearly IC divided by its standard error across years, so it measures the consistency of the ranking skill.

Paired across years against the fair \texttt{structured [ridge]} baseline, \texttt{struct+tfidf} adds $+0.016$ IC ($p=0.098$) on the seven-year window and $+0.011$ IC ($p=0.057$) on the twelve-year window. In incremental-value terms this is the clearest statement of the study's central finding: text adds a small, positive, mostly consistent amount over the structured baseline, on top of a baseline whose own superiority over pure persistence is unambiguous ($+0.059$ IC over lagged, $p=0.007$). The increment is just short of the conventional significance threshold on twelve years of data. This is the honest shape of the headline result, and Section~\ref{sec:horizon} asks whether it is a property of the 30-day horizon or holds more generally.

\subsection{The admissibility audit}
Before running encoder backtests, the training provenance of each encoder was checked against the backtest years. This check found that the two task-aligned encoders could not be legitimately backtested with their existing checkpoints. \texttt{ftvol} was trained on the volatility labels of all pre-2025 filings, and its best epoch was chosen using validation-year IC. \texttt{volaware}'s contrastive pairing used within-year forward-volatility deciles computed across all pre-2025 filings. Both practices mean the same checkpoint that would be tested on, say, 2018 had already seen 2018 labels during its own training. This is lookahead leakage, even though the downstream prediction head is trained cleanly on each backtest year. It is the same temporal-leakage failure mode the methodology literature warns about \cite{de2018advances}, but one level removed: the leak sits inside the text representation rather than inside the predictor. For the same reason, change features derived from these two encoders' embeddings are excluded from every backtest. As a result, the in-period parity of \texttt{ftvol} and \texttt{volaware} reported in Section~\ref{sec:results-primary} could be neither confirmed nor refuted by any backtest using the existing checkpoints, and a retrain under a clean protocol was required.

\subsection{The clean-protocol retrain and the final verdict}
The decisive experiment retrains the supervised encoder under a strict temporal cutoff. Training uses only filings from before 2017, the best epoch is chosen on 2017, and the resulting checkpoint is then frozen. Years 2018--2024 are never touched by the encoder in any way, which makes the 2018--2024 backtest admissible by construction. The retrained encoder (\texttt{ftvol2018}) is strong in-period, with a filing-level IC of 0.635 on its 2017 selection year from text alone, confirming that the supervised objective learns a real signal.

Out of period, the encoder's out-of-period advantage disappears entirely. Its embeddings add nothing over the structured features alone, since the difference is statistically zero, and it loses to the count model by about 0.06 IC. Combined with the encoder grid, this gives the verdict of the whole study: \textbf{no encoder configuration beats the count-based model under any admissible protocol.} Stated as a finding rather than a contest, a simple lexical representation remains the stronger choice out of sample whenever the alternative is a dense encoder that must fix its text-to-volatility mapping on a bounded historical window. Task-aligned training reaches parity within its own training period, but that parity does not survive forward transfer, so the conditions under which count-based text stays competitive are precisely the forward-looking, non-stationary ones that matter for deployment. The apparent parity of the original \texttt{ftvol} on the validation year is now explained. It came from training on all pre-2025 labels plus epoch selection on the evaluation year, and the clean protocol removes both of these advantages.

The result also carries a positive characterisation, which is the more interesting contribution. Task-aligned training closes the gap in-period, but that advantage does not survive forward transfer. A plausible reading, offered as an interpretation the evidence supports rather than a demonstrated mechanism, is that the dense text-to-volatility mapping the encoder learns is consistent with an era-specific relationship between the training era's risk vocabulary and volatility, one that drifts over time. Under that reading, the TF-IDF lanes are not immune to drift because bag-of-words is a better representation in any single year. They are immune because the lexical features are transparent levels, and an annually refit linear head can re-weight them each year at negligible cost. This reading is consistent with the concept-drift framework \cite{lu2018learning} and with the time-varying text-return relationships documented on this disclosure section by Magner et al.\ \cite{magner2025decoding}, though the study has not isolated the drift mechanism directly, for instance by measuring the year-to-year stability of the learned text-to-volatility map itself, so it remains the best-supported interpretation rather than an established fact.

One point about the comparison is worth noting: the frozen \texttt{ftvol2018} checkpoint is up to eight years older than the TF-IDF lanes, which are refit every year (see Limitations, Chapter~\ref{sec:discussion}). Even the one case with no staleness at all, the original \texttt{ftvol} evaluated on 2025, only ties with the count model rather than beating it, so this age gap is unlikely to change the verdict.

This is the opposite of the naive expectation that a slow-moving annual disclosure should matter more at longer horizons. The resolution is visible in the overall predictability column: persistence alone climbs from 0.193 to 0.534 as the horizon lengthens, because longer realised-volatility windows are smoother and increasingly dominated by the persistent component of volatility, which is precisely what the structured block's multi-horizon trailing measures already carry. What the text contributes appears to be the transient, near-filing component of uncertainty, which washes out of the target as the horizon lengthens.

This evidence should be read with one qualification stated plainly: eight horizons were swept without a multiplicity adjustment, so the individual $p$-values at three, five and seven days are not, on their own, a five-percent-level claim. \section{Disclosure-change features (the Lazy-Prices branch)}
\label{sec:results-change}
Cohen, Malloy and Nguyen \cite{cohen2020lazy} showed that year-over-year changes in 10-K text predict returns. In their account, the alpha comes from investors under-reacting to the changes that do occur. Kravet and Muslu \cite{kravet2013textual} report the volatility-side analogue of this effect. This corpus shows a similar pattern of persistence, which a stale-text experiment quantifies directly. Roughly three quarters of the filings were re-paired with the adjacent year's risk text instead of their own, and the \texttt{struct+tfidf} validation IC was essentially unchanged (0.611 against 0.610). Year-stale risk text therefore predicts volatility almost exactly as well as current risk text. This means the text signal identified in Section~\ref{sec:results-primary} sits in the persistent \emph{level} of the risk language rather than in how recently it was written, which agrees with Campbell et al.\ \cite{campbell2014information}. This persistence sharpens the question for the rest of this section: if the level of risk language barely moves from year to year, do the small movements that do occur carry any additional information about volatility?

To test this, a set of change features was built for each consecutive firm-year pair, covering 6{,}713 firm-year pairs in total. The features are: TF-IDF cosine similarity between the two filings, embedding cosine similarity under the admissible encoders, Jaccard overlap of the text, the ratio of filing lengths, and the fraction of paragraphs in the later filing with no close match in the earlier one. These features pass a simple construction check: median lexical similarity dips visibly in fiscal 2020 (0.964, against roughly 0.976 in normal years), which is exactly where the COVID-19 rewrite of risk sections should show up.

Adding change features to the count model performs slightly worse than the count model alone on both windows (paired differences of $-0.011$ and $-0.007$, $p=0.37$ and $0.28$), and change features without TF-IDF perform worse than the structured baseline. Disclosure change, whether measured lexically or semantically, adds no incremental volatility signal beyond the level of the text. This null result is consistent with the source literature. The Lazy-Prices effect is specifically a \emph{returns} phenomenon, driven by investors under-reacting to changes in the text. For volatility, by contrast, the informative quantity is the level of risk language rather than its year-over-year shift.

\section{Earnings-call tone: a horizon-contrast finding}
\label{sec:results-calls}
Stage~C asks whether earnings-call tone, the second text modality described in Section~\ref{sec:calls-method}, adds signal over the structured baseline and the 10-K text already in the panel. The question was answered in two steps at two different anchors, and the contrast between them is the finding.

The first step was call-anchored. A crude test of tone against persistence, taken linearly, found nothing. Re-tested inside the full model (structured block plus tone under an HGB head, leave-one-quarter-out), tone adds $+0.039$ IC over structured alone (0.594 against 0.555), roughly the same increment that 10-K text adds. The methodological lesson stands regardless of anchor: a feature can add nothing on its own and still add real value in combination, so incremental tests must be run inside the full model.

The second step uses the full transcript history at the filing anchor, where tone is attached to each filing from the most recent preceding call. Two independent evaluations were run. Within the 2025 validation cross-section, a five-fold cross-validated comparison with identical folds and identical heads per pair found every tone increment flat or negative (for example, $-0.017$ IC under the HGB head on the full panel). Every confidence interval straddles zero, and the one significant accuracy test favours the model without tone. Across the 2018--2024 expanding-window backtest, where historical coverage makes calls a fully backtestable family, structured plus tone matches structured alone (0.515 against 0.518, paired $p=0.395$) and the full model with tone matches it without (0.561 against 0.561, $p=0.436$).

A natural objection to this filing-anchor null is that it might be an artefact of the 30-day label rather than a genuine absence of signal at a horizon the study happens not to measure. The horizon spectrum of Section~\ref{sec:results-horizon} answers this at no additional data cost. Across the full spectrum from three to ninety days, adding tone to the structured model produces increments of $+0.011$, $+0.006$, $-0.003$, $-0.001$, $-0.006$, $-0.005$, $+0.002$ and $-0.003$, and adding it to the full model with 10-K text produces increments whose absolute value never exceeds $0.001$ at any horizon. Stage~C's filing-level null therefore holds at every horizon, not only the one at which it was first stated. One further observation is worth recording: the short-horizon flicker appears over the structured model alone and vanishes over the full model, which suggests that where tone carries anything at all at the filing anchor, the 10-K's own text has already captured it. As second text modalities go, calls behave as a substitute for the filing text rather than a complement to it.

At the call anchor the same horizon sweep can be run, and is reported as the pilot it is, on 152 filings in a single 2025 regime. Tone's increment over the structured model traces a hump across the spectrum, negative at three days, then $+0.079$, $+0.084$, $+0.142$ and $+0.134$ at five, seven, ten and twenty days, $+0.039$ at thirty, and negative again at sixty and ninety. The headline $+0.039$ at thirty days therefore sits on the decaying shoulder of that hump rather than at its peak, so the call-anchored effect, such as it is, behaves as a two-to-four-week phenomenon rather than a monthly one. At this sample size the curve is jagged, and the shape is the most that can be claimed from it.

Both the filing-anchor and call-anchor results are correct. They measure different horizons. A plausible reading, offered as an interpretation the results support rather than an established mechanism, is that management tone carries genuine volatility information at the call date, but that by the filing date, one to three months later, the structured block has already absorbed it, since the realised-volatility windows measured at filing span exactly the post-call period the tone predicted. Stated as a finding, Stage~C identifies the condition under which a second text modality stops adding value: an anchor placed after the market has had time to impound the signal into the structured features that are cheaper to compute and already refit annually.

\section{Insider trading and the risk text: when the text increment is earned}
\label{sec:results-insider}
Stage~E asks the question Section~\ref{sec:insider-method} sets up: does the text increment established in Section~\ref{sec:results-robust} concentrate in a specific part of the cross-section, conditioned on pre-filing insider-trading activity?

The level question is answered first, and its answer is null. Adding the insider block to the structured model changes nothing in either backtest window (paired $\Delta$IC $+0.000$ at $p=0.897$ over seven years, $+0.001$ at $p=0.768$ over twelve years), and adding it to the full model likewise ($+0.000$ at $p=0.886$ and $-0.001$ at $p=0.585$). The 2025 validation rows agree. This is consistent with the study's own architecture: the structured block already contains realised volatility, drawdown and liquidity measures, and whatever elevated insider activity accompanies, those features appear to reflect already.

The conditioning question, pre-registered with a directional hypothesis before the run, was tested on two conditioners, and they initially appeared to answer in opposite directions. Conditioned on insider \emph{disagreement}, the hypothesis holds: the increment is larger where trading insiders are split (high-minus-low $\Delta$IC of $+0.029$, $t=+2.04$, $p=0.088$ over seven years, positive in six of seven; $+0.016$ at $p=0.158$ over twelve). Conditioned on abnormal trading \emph{intensity}, the increment initially appeared larger in the low-intensity half ($-0.012$, $t=-2.67$, $p=0.037$ over seven years, negative in six of seven).

That second result does not survive further scrutiny, and is withdrawn rather than quietly dropped. Re-running the conditioning test at the seven further horizons of Section~\ref{sec:results-horizon} places the intensity effect at $-0.001$, $-0.005$, $-0.008$, $-0.009$, $-0.007$, $+0.002$ and $+0.013$ across three to ninety days: no cell significant, none larger in magnitude than 0.013, and the sign turning positive at both ends of the spectrum. The nominally significant thirty-day cell has no support from any adjacent horizon, the signature of a single-cell finding in a family that is otherwise null. Its counterpart is credentialled by the same discipline that retires it: insider disagreement is corroborated at twenty days ($+0.029$) as well as at thirty ($+0.030$), agreement between neighbouring horizons that is the evidence the thirty-day disagreement cell is not isolated.

A plausible reading of what remains, offered as an interpretation the results support rather than an established mechanism, is that disagreement marks unresolved dispersion among the best-informed parties, precisely the state in which narrative disclosure would still carry incremental information, and the state that Shalen~\cite{shalen1993volume} models as generating additional volatility directly. Read this way, the insider-conditioning result extends the same absorption reading that Section~\ref{sec:results-calls} establishes in time to the cross-section: the text increment is earned on the firms whose information the market has not yet impounded. The bounds of this surviving claim are stated plainly. Two conditioners were tested over two windows and eight horizons with no multiplicity adjustment. The disagreement effect attenuates as the backtest extends back to 2013, concentrating from 2018 onward. Its strongest $p$-value is 0.088, and the disagreement high-cell is small, 52 to 89 firms per year. Stage~E therefore closes as a null level effect together with suggestive, horizon-corroborated evidence that the text increment is state-dependent, largest where informed parties disagree.

\section{Synthesis: the defended benchmark}
\label{sec:results-synth}
The final model of the study is the structured feature block plus full-text TF-IDF under a sparse ridge head. Its performance is strong and consistent: a mean IC of 0.614 ($t=10.4$) over the twelve-year expanding-window backtest, and an $R^2_{\log}$ of 0.226 on the 2025 validation year. It also carries a small text increment over the fair structured baseline of $+0.011$ IC ($p=0.057$), together with a decisively significant gain in accuracy, and the model ranking behind that increment is unchanged across eight measurement horizons (Section~\ref{sec:results-horizon}).

This model was tested against every challenger fielded across the study: two prediction heads, eight text representations, three pooling schemes, five encoders (including a modern general-purpose embedder and two task-supervised ones), topic exposures, change features, call-tone features, and insider-trading features, individually and in full fusion models. All of these comparisons were run under leakage-audited conditions, extending even to the training provenance of the encoders themselves, and the defended model beat or matched every one of them. In incremental-value terms, every comparison in this chapter answers the same question stated in Chapter~\ref{sec:sota}: not which representation, anchor, or firm subset ranks best on its own, but how much each adds over what the structured baseline already knows. The answer, examined from five separate directions, converges on the same shape: a small, real, level-persistent increment from the filing's own text, absent from a second text modality once it has had time to be absorbed, and concentrated, when present at all from a third information source, in the part of the cross-section where the market has not yet caught up.

\chapter{Discussion and Conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Contributions.} This study makes seven contributions that rest on direct evidence, and an eighth addressed by a bounded pilot rather than by argument alone. Each rests on a broad, carefully controlled evaluation rather than a single comparison. First, it turns the original one-year finding, that TF-IDF beats every learned encoder, into an evaluated benchmark: the same conclusion now stands up against a wide grid of alternatives across twelve years of walk-forward backtests and eight measurement horizons. That grid is deliberately exhaustive, spanning non-text baselines (persistence, structured financial features), simple-text baselines (TF-IDF and its Loughran--McDonald-calibrated reading), and representation baselines (SBERT, BGE, the contrastive encoders, and topic exposures), all scored under the same incremental-value test and the same fixed prediction head. Second, the study offers a plausible account of \emph{why} the count-based model wins. The volatility signal in risk-factor text is lexical and persistent, and although a task-aligned encoder can learn this signal within its own training period, the mapping it learns is specific to that period and does not carry over to later years. To our knowledge, this is the first time a volatility-supervised text encoder has been tested this honestly outside its training period, though the drift mechanism itself has not been isolated directly, and the account is offered as the best-supported interpretation rather than a demonstrated fact. Third, the study establishes a persistence finding: risk-factor text from the previous year predicts volatility almost as well as the current year's text. Fourth, it reports a null result with an explanation, namely that changes in disclosure text do not improve volatility prediction, and the contrast with the Lazy-Prices literature on returns explains why. Fifth, relabelling the task across eight horizons from three to ninety trading days shows the model ranking is horizon-robust while the text increment itself is single-peaked at seven days and gone by sixty, reaching conventional significance at the short end where the 2020 regime break contributes least variance to the panel. Sixth, a horizon-contrast finding for a second text modality: earnings-call tone predicts volatility at the call anchor yet adds nothing at the filing anchor, in 2025 and across seven backtest years and all eight horizons, a plausible reading being that the signal is absorbed into the structured features during the call-to-filing gap. Seventh, conditioning the text increment on pre-filing insider-trading activity indicates it concentrates in firms whose insiders disagree, corroborated across two adjacent horizons. The conditioning design itself, using an insider-trading state to locate where in the cross-section a text increment lives rather than only whether it exists on average, is a contribution in its own right, since no precedent for it was found in the course of this study's literature review. Eighth, a bounded pilot on generative feature extraction finds that an open-weights language model's own reading of the insider-trading record is statistically indistinguishable from the eight hand-crafted features summarising the same record, and that the model's elicited scores are themselves unstable across repeated identical queries. This negative result was obtained directly rather than argued from precedent, and Section~\ref{sec:llm-route} states it in full. Alongside these findings, the study produces a reusable firm-filing feature table as a standalone deliverable, so that the underlying representations can be reused directly without repeating the full pipeline.

\textbf{Limitations.} The results have several limitations, none of which undermines the headline findings but each of which bounds how far they can be pushed. The text increment on ranking accuracy is small and, while positive and consistent across most years and horizons, its statistical significance at the 30-day headline horizon is still borderline ($p=0.057$ over twelve years). The horizon spectrum shows this resolves by shortening the horizon rather than by accumulating further years at the original one, so the two are not independent tests of the same open question. The clean-protocol retrain compares a frozen 2018 encoder against text representations that are refit every year, which gives the encoder an unavoidable staleness disadvantage of one to eight years. Retraining the encoder inside every backtest window would remove this disadvantage but requires twelve separate GPU fine-tunes, which was outside the scope of the current study. The eight-horizon sweep that underlies both the term-structure finding and the insider-conditioning corroboration was run without a multiplicity adjustment, so individual horizon-level $p$-values should be read as corroborating a shape rather than as independent significance claims. The insider-disagreement conditioning result rests on a small high-activity cell (52 to 89 firms per year), attenuates when the backtest extends back to 2013, and sits alongside a second conditioning result that did not survive the same horizon check and was withdrawn. Both facts bound how much weight the surviving result can carry. The level-based $R^2$ metric is noisy in individual backtest years and occasionally negative, which is why the ranking-based information coefficient is used as the primary and more reliable measure throughout. Finally, the generative-feature-extraction pilot of Section~\ref{sec:llm-route} is bounded by design to 2024 and 2025 filings, so its contamination probe is a placebo check on a post-cutoff slice and says nothing directly about the years, 2018 to 2024, where the contamination objection would actually apply.

\textbf{Future work.} Several directions follow directly from these findings and limitations. As more evaluation years accumulate, the text increment over the structured baseline at the 30-day headline horizon can be re-tested to see whether it independently crosses the conventional significance threshold, complementing rather than substituting for the horizon-based resolution already found. A longer-term extension would retrain the task-aligned encoder inside each backtest window, removing the staleness handicap entirely and testing directly whether more frequent retraining can close the out-of-period gap, or whether the era-specific mapping problem is more fundamental than retraining frequency can fix. Benchmarking the \texttt{dual} replication directly against Chiu et al.\ \cite{chiu2025financial} on their own retrieval task, rather than only on the volatility task used here, would clarify how much of the encoder grid's performance reflects the retrieval-style contrastive objective itself. On the call-tone finding, the natural next step is a shorter call-to-filing anchor, informed directly by the horizon result: since the call-anchored effect peaks at ten to twenty days, a mid-quarter anchor paired with a label of that length is a better-targeted test than the study's own thirty-day headline window. On the insider-conditioning result, a natural next step is to test whether the same state-dependence replicates under other information-environment conditioners, most plausibly news-coverage intensity and news-tone dispersion, run across the full horizon spectrum from the outset, since a single-horizon replication would not have been able to separate a genuine effect from the single-cell artefact this study already retired. This direction was assessed for inclusion in the present study and declined on two grounds rather than left as an oversight: the natural news corpus identifies firms only by free-text organisation name, with no CIK or ticker, so linking it to this study's panel would require a temporally valid name-to-firm bridge, itself a project-sized prerequisite given how far a subtle join defect was shown to propagate in Section~\ref{sec:results-robust}'s admissibility audit. The natural corpus's coverage also begins only in 2013, which would foreclose the twelve-year backtest every other result in this study reports alongside the seven-year window, breaking the cross-stage comparability the evaluation protocol is built on. It is recorded here as specified future work rather than as an untested idea.

\section{The generative-language-model route}
\label{sec:llm-route}
Every text representation evaluated in the main study is an \emph{encoder}: TF-IDF, SBERT, BGE, the DAPT model, and the two task-aligned encoders. A distinct use of language models, prompting a \emph{generative} model to score a document on interpretable dimensions and using those scores as dense features, is untested by that pipeline. It is the natural next question, and it differs from the encoder route in a way that could matter: the output is low-dimensional and interpretable, so it might resist the era-specific drift that Section~\ref{sec:results-robust} finds defeats the dense encoders, in the way lexicon-based tone features do. This section records why the route is nevertheless treated as out of scope for the main study, states the reasoning as far as it can currently be supported by published-venue literature, and reports a bounded pilot run to test the position against evidence rather than assert it.

\textbf{Score stability, measured directly.} Before turning to the wider literature, it is worth reporting what this study's own pilot observed. An open-weights instruction model was asked to score 865 insider-trading windows on three zero-to-hundred dimensions, five independent times per filing, under a fixed prompt, a fixed model version and a fixed decoding seed, conditions as favourable to stability as a real deployment could realistically offer. Every one of the 4,325 resulting responses parsed successfully, so what follows is not a formatting artefact. The median standard deviation across the five re-runs of the \emph{same} prompt on the \emph{same} filing was 59\% of the standard deviation across \emph{different} filings on the volatility-risk dimension, 88\% on information asymmetry, and 96\% on the model's own stated confidence. On the last of these the elicited score is very nearly pure noise: re-asking the identical question about one company moves the answer almost as much as switching to a different company entirely. Averaging the five draws recovers almost none of this loss, lifting the resulting information coefficient from 0.733 to only 0.736. A usable feature must be a comparatively fixed measurement. A quantity whose re-measurement error approaches its cross-sectional signal is not one.

This directly measured instability is consistent with, and anticipated by, published findings on the same class of models. Sclar et al.\ \cite{sclar2024quantifying} find open-source language models extremely sensitive to prompt formatting that is explicitly meaning-preserving, with performance differences of up to 76 accuracy points from typesetting an unchanged question differently. Ouyang et al.\ \cite{ouyang2025empirical} find that identical requests to the same model disagree on a large majority of code-generation tasks, and that fixing the decoding temperature to zero reduces but does not eliminate this non-determinism, a finding cited here for the decoding mechanism it establishes rather than for a magnitude specific to numeric scoring. Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2024large} show that a language model used as an evaluator can have its ranking of two candidate responses reversed simply by reordering them, adjacent evidence for scorer instability in a pairwise rather than absolute-scoring setting.

\textbf{Why backtest contamination is weighted heavily.} A second, independent concern applies specifically to using a generative model to score historical filings inside a backtest: if the model's training data overlaps the years being evaluated, any resulting skill estimate may be inflated by the model's prior knowledge of what actually happened, rather than by genuine inference from the text. This study is well placed to take that concern seriously, because it has already measured the price of an analogous form of leakage on its own encoder route: Section~\ref{sec:results-robust} finds that an encoder trained on all pre-2025 labels and epoch-selected on the evaluation year shows roughly 0.06 IC of apparent skill that disappears entirely once training is restricted to a strict pre-cutoff window, several times the size of the genuine text increment the study defends. That leakage was fixable because the encoder could be retrained under a clean temporal cutoff and re-evaluated. A generative model's training run is not something this study can repeat on a rolling window, so the equivalent fix is not available for the generative route in the same way. A route whose central validity threat this study can measure precisely, on its own data, but cannot remove the same way, is a reasonable one to treat cautiously pending a fuller finance-specific literature review of the contamination mechanism, which is being finalised separately and was not complete at the time of writing.

\textbf{The bounded pilot, and what it settles.} Declining a direction on argument alone invites the reply that the argument is a rationalisation, so the cheapest decisive version of the experiment was run before the position was fixed, with its evaluation design and stopping rule written down before any score was examined. Scope was chosen so that the contamination concern could not apply to the pilot itself: only 2024 and 2025 filings were scored, both post-dating the scoring model's training cutoff, and every prompt was identifier-stripped, carrying no ticker, company name, insider name, or absolute date, only day offsets relative to the filing and pseudonymised insider labels. The model read the same pre-filing transaction window that the eight hand-crafted Form~4 features summarise, and its three scores were compared against those features under one prediction head and one set of folds, in the same five-fold within-year design used for the earnings-call filing-anchor test, for the same reason: a score that exists only on the evaluation rows cannot be learned under a train-before-year split.

The comparison is a null in every direction that matters. Against the structured baseline (information coefficient 0.736), the eight hand-crafted features give 0.730 and the model's own scores give 0.736. The difference between the two feature blocks is $+0.006$, with a paired bootstrap interval of $[-0.000, +0.013]$, inside the half-width its own stopping rule specified in advance. The finding is not that the language model reads Form~4 records badly. It is that feature engineering was never the binding constraint on this record. A hand-built summary and a generative model's own reading of the same raw data are statistically indistinguishable from each other, and both are statistically indistinguishable from omitting insider information altogether, which is exactly what the level test of Section~\ref{sec:results-insider} already found over seven and twelve backtest years. What appears to bind is the information content of the record relative to a structured block that already carries realised volatility, drawdown and liquidity.

One further reading deserves care, because it would be easy to overclaim. Scoring the identified prompts as a control produced an information coefficient of 0.737 against the anonymised pilot's 0.736, a premium of $+0.001$ with an interval of $[-0.004, +0.006]$. On a post-cutoff slice there should be no look-ahead advantage to find, so this is a placebo check that passed, evidence that the pilot's own scoping decision worked as intended, not a measurement showing contamination to be small in general. The years where the contamination concern would actually apply, 2018 to 2024, are outside the pilot's scope by construction, and the pilot says nothing directly about them. A fuller treatment of the generative-LLM route, including a complete finance-specific literature grounding of the contamination mechanism, calibration and stability analysis across model versions, and inference over the full filing corpus for at least two model families under the admissibility discipline of Section~\ref{sec:results-robust}, is identified here as a direction for future research rather than as an extension of the present study. What this study contributes to it is a negative result obtained cheaply and a stability measurement obtained directly on real financial records.

\chapter{Structured feature definitions}
\label{app:features}
The structured block (\S\ref{sec:struct}) comprises: realised volatility at 21/63/126/252 trading-day horizons (HAR-RV \cite{corsi2009simple}); market beta and idiosyncratic volatility relative to a factor model \cite{ang2006cross}; log market capitalisation (size), leverage, book-to-market \cite{fama1992cross,ang2006cross} and return on assets (Compustat); Amihud illiquidity as $|R|/$dollar-volume \cite{amihud2002illiquidity}; return skewness and kurtosis \cite{conrad2013ex} (kurtosis exploratory); past-12-month momentum \cite{ang2006cross}; and vol-of-vol, drawdown and dollar volume. Coverage is approximately 99\% across 7{,}666 filings.

\chapter{Training recipes and hyperparameters}
\label{app:recipes}
\emph{DAPT:} \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2}; continued MLM at 15\% masking; learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$; effective batch 32; five epochs; early stopping on validation perplexity; fp16; 512-token, paragraph-aware, non-overlapping packing. \emph{Contrastive:} SimCSE-style InfoNCE, in-batch negatives, temperature 0.05; three views with the soft sector view down-weighted 0.5; label-aware false-negative masking on the 2-digit SIC label; \texttt{[NUM]}/\texttt{[DATE]} normalisation; 256-token paragraphs; LoRA rank 16 for \texttt{three\_lora}. \emph{Windowed encoding:} 256-token windows, stride 192, mean-pool to a 768-d paragraph vector; 462{,}590 windows over 176{,}616 paragraphs. \emph{BERTopic:} UMAP (15 neighbours, 5 components, cosine) $\rightarrow$ HDBSCAN (minimum cluster size 200) $\rightarrow$ class-based TF-IDF, 49 topics. \emph{Backtest:} expanding window, test years 2013--2024 and 2018--2024; clean-protocol retrain (\texttt{ftvol2018}) trained on years before 2017, epoch-selected on 2017, then frozen. \emph{Horizon spectrum:} labels regenerated at 3, 5, 7, 10, 20, 60 and 90 trading days, construction validated against exact reproduction of the 30-day labels before use. \emph{Insider-trading features:} trailing window 180 to 3 days before filing; opportunistic-trade threshold of fewer than three prior years with a same-calendar-month trade, not required to be consecutive. \emph{Generative pilot:} five re-runs per filing at fixed prompt, model version and decoding seed; identifier-stripped prompts scoped to 2024--2025 filings only.

\chapter{Firm-filing feature-table schema}
\label{app:schema}
The reusable deliverable ($D_4$) is a firm-filing table, one row per filing: \texttt{CIK} (EDGAR), \texttt{PERMNO} (CRSP CCM linkage), \texttt{filing\_date} (EDGAR submissions API), \texttt{SIC2} (2-digit industry), the dense risk embedding (768-d, from the contrastive encoder), the topic-exposure vector (49-d, from BERTopic), \texttt{lagged\_vol\_30d} (CRSP), and \texttt{fwd\_vol\_30d} (the label; CRSP for the full sample). The first four columns are identifiers, the next two are produced by the NLP pipeline, and the last two are financial labels, so one row is a self-contained record for reproducing or extending the experiments.

\chapter{Generative feature-extraction pilot: technical detail}
\label{app:llm-pilot}
This appendix reports the full technical detail behind the pilot summarised in Section~\ref{sec:llm-route}. The pilot scored 865 insider-trading windows on three zero-to-hundred dimensions (volatility-risk, information asymmetry, and the model's own stated confidence), five independent times per filing under a fixed prompt, model version and decoding seed, for 4,325 total responses, every one of which parsed successfully. The median ratio of within-filing (same prompt, same filing, across the five re-runs) to between-filing standard deviation was 59\% on the volatility-risk dimension, 88\% on information asymmetry, and 96\% on the model's own stated confidence; averaging the five draws per filing raised the resulting information coefficient only from 0.733 to 0.736.

The main comparison, on the identifier-stripped, 2024--2025-only pilot scope, evaluated three feature blocks against the structured baseline (information coefficient 0.736) under one prediction head and one set of five-fold within-year cross-validation folds: the eight hand-crafted Form~4 features (0.730), and the model's own three scores (0.736). The difference between the two feature blocks is $+0.006$, with a paired bootstrap interval of $[-0.000, +0.013]$, inside the half-width set by the pilot's pre-registered stopping rule. A control run on the same 2024--2025 scope with real identifiers retained (ticker, company name, insider names, absolute dates) produced an information coefficient of 0.737 against the anonymised pilot's 0.736, a premium of $+0.001$ with an interval of $[-0.004, +0.006]$, the expected result of a placebo check on a post-cutoff slice where no look-ahead advantage should exist to find.
